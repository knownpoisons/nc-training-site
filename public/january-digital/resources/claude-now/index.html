01  Master prompt one

The Panel.

A room full of experts, on tap. The human 10% in.

Ask Claude one question and you get one answer — the safe, averaged one. So don't ask one voice. Convene a panel of experts, the same ones every time, and let them fight over what you're building. Then make them finish the job: the panel agrees a ranking system, scores every element, delivers the findings — and the chair wraps it into a report. What everyone agrees you should do from here, where they split, and the red flags. Not five opinions — a decision.

When to reach for it
Pressure-testing a concept before it goes near a client · breaking open a stuck idea · getting a caption or hook reframed five ways · sanity-checking a pitch for the hole you can't see.

The master prompt

You are a standing panel of five experts reviewing something I'm building. Each of you reviews it from your own corner, in your own voice, and then hands it back to me reframed the way YOU would do it. You are allowed — encouraged — to disagree with each other. Generic praise is useless to me; be specific and be honest. THE PANEL - The Performance Marketer — only cares whether it stops the scroll and converts. Ruthless about the first second. - The Creative Director — only cares about the idea. Will kill anything that's just a nice-looking asset with no thought behind it. - The Brand Guardian — knows the guidelines cold and knows exactly what the client will never sign off. - The Sceptical Client — busy, a little threatened, looking for the reason to say no. - The Customer — the actual person being sold to. Owes you nothing. Would scroll straight past unless something earns the stop. RUN IT IN FOUR STAGES, in this order: STAGE 1 — THE PANEL SPEAKS For each panellist, in turn: 1. THE READ — their honest first reaction, one or two lines. 2. THE PROBLEM — the single biggest thing they'd change, and why. 3. THE REFRAME — how they'd do it instead. Show it, don't just describe it. STAGE 2 — THE RANKING The panel now sets up a ranking system together: - Break what I gave you into its distinct elements (the concepts, the sections, the lines — whatever the natural units are, name them). - Agree 3–5 criteria that fit what this thing has to do. - Each panellist scores every element against every criterion, 1–5, from their own corner. No inflation — a 5 is rare. - Combine into one ranking table, best to worst, with each panellist's scores visible so I can see WHO rated WHAT. STAGE 3 — THE FINDINGS Deliver the findings plainly: which elements won and why, which lost and why, and where the panel genuinely split — with the reason for the split, not just the fact of it. STAGE 4 — THE CHAIR'S REPORT Wrap it up as the chair. This is the part I act on: - DO FROM HERE — what everyone agrees should be done, as a numbered list, in order. - THE SPLITS — where the panel disagrees, and which way you'd lean as chair, with the reason. - RED FLAGS — anything that could embarrass us or get rejected: brand risk, claim risk, an AI tell, a client-will-never-sign-off problem. Even small ones. - WORTH KNOWING — anything extra the panel spotted along the way that didn't fit above. - THE ONE MOVE — the single highest-leverage change to make right now. HERE'S WHAT I'M BUILDING: [ paste your concept / caption / pitch / brief — or several options, numbered ]

Swap the five voices per brand — a beauty client's panel isn't a workwear client's panel. Build it once, keep it under the keyboard.

"You will feel so much more confident — and get much better at setting feedback — if you run this process and then do your own round of revisions before you send it to them."— Jeremy, Session 09
02  Master prompt two

The Scorer.

Nothing leaves the building unscored. The human 10% out.

You've made twenty concepts. Which two go to the client? Not by eye at 6pm with your own bias baked in. A scorer — a second pass whose only job is to grade and rank what you made against the brief. The criteria are your constraints: the C in ICC becomes the rubric. And when the client asks “why these two?”, you're not saying “we liked them” — it's scored against their own brief.

The master prompt

You are a scorer. Your only job is to score and rank what I give you against the brief. You are not here to be encouraging or to hedge. You are here to tell me which ones are actually good, which ones only look good, and why. A top score is rare — do not inflate. THE BRIEF / THE CRITERIA [ paste the brief, or list 4–6 criteria. For ads, usually: on-brief · on-brand · stops the scroll · one clear idea · would convert · no AI tells ] WHAT TO DO 1. Score each option against each criterion, 1–5, with a one-line reason for each score. 2. Total and rank them, best to worst, in a clean table. 3. Name the top two, and in one line each say what earned them the spot. 4. Flag the traps — anything that looks strong but misses the brief. Say what it's missing. 5. Name the single most common weakness across the whole set, so I know what to fix next round. 6. Then write me the Flora change prompts — the exact edits that would take each weak element as close to top marks as possible, ready to paste straight into the canvas. If none of them are good enough to ship, say so plainly and tell me why. HERE ARE THE OUTPUTS: [ paste them, numbered ]

Feed it the real brief — the better the criteria, the sharper the scoring. First job: run it on your own competition entries and see if it agrees with the room.

"The quality of your work is defined by the quality of the questions that you ask your Claudette."— Jeremy, Session 09
Panel vs Scorer — which one when
The Panel ranks from five human corners before you build — perspectives in. The Scorer grades coldly against the brief before it ships — compliance out. Chain them for the full loop: Panel breaks the idea open, the machine produces at volume, the Scorer picks the winners.

03  The pipeline

Connectors — feedback reads itself.

The Session 6 rule, with the manual step removed.

The rule was: feedback goes to Claude to decipher before you read or watch it. The weak point was the copy-paste — on a busy Tuesday, you'd skip it. Connectors remove the step. Claude connects to Drive and Slack and reads the client's comments at the source. The rule becomes the default instead of a discipline.

The decode prompt

Read the client feedback on [document name] in my Google Drive. Then give me: 1. WHAT THEY ACTUALLY WANT — the real asks, decoded, stripped of politeness and vagueness. 2. IN-SCOPE vs OUT — which asks are round-one changes vs genuine errors vs new requests that sit outside the agreed round (per our process rules). 3. THE CANVAS LIST — the changes as concrete prompt edits, ready to feed into Flora. 4. THE REPLY — a short, warm draft back to the client confirming what we're doing and (politely) what sits outside this round.

Point 2 holds the boundary for you — it applies the round rules to every piece of feedback automatically.

04  Memory

Past chats — the scars layer builds itself.

The fifth layer everyone skips, maintained for free.

Claude can now search your past conversations and remember context across a project. The scars layer — what we tried, what broke, what we decided and why — stops depending on anyone's discipline. It accumulates just by you working.

The two prompts

What did we decide about [the thing] — and why? Search our past chats on this.
Before I start this brief: search our past conversations on [brand] and give me the scars — what we tried that didn't work, what the client rejected, and any rules we set. I don't want to repeat a mistake we've already paid for.

Make the second one a habit at the top of every new brief. Thirty seconds — the difference between a team that learns and a team that re-learns.

05  On the move

Voice — walk the brief.

The Session 3 technique, untethered from the desk.

Voice mode on the phone is good enough to do real work now. AirPods in, leave the screen, and talk the brief through — what the client said, what's bothering you, what the idea might be. Claude asks back, pushes, structures. By the time you're back at the desk, the thinking's done.

Say this to open

I'm going to talk through a brief out loud while I walk — messy, unstructured, thinking as I go. Your job: ask me the questions that sharpen it, challenge anything vague, and at the end give me back a structured brief in our format — role, task, context, tone, reference, deliverable, refinement. Don't let me get away with "good vibes" as a direction.

Best for the brief you're avoiding. Twenty minutes of walking beats ninety at the desk.

06  The point

All of it feeds the loop.

The programme built you a loop — data in, creative out, results back. These remove the manual labour around it: skills produce the documents, connectors carry the feedback, memory keeps the scars, voice does the thinking on the move, and the Panel and Scorer guard the two ends. The loop is the advantage. This makes the loop cheaper to run.

07  The rule

Never settle for the first answer.

"Don't ever accept the first thing that she brings back to you. Assume that you didn't ask the right amount of information."— Jeremy, Session 09

The quality of your Claude output — and everything that comes out of you on the back of it — is dictated by the questions you ask. Push it to think laterally. Give it references. Ask it to suggest ideas and let your brain riff on them. The prompts on this page are starting positions, not finish lines.
