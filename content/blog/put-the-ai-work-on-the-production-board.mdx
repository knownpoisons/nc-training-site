A few weeks into the Maesa engagement, I sat in on a launch status meeting. The project tracker was up on the screen, every line had an owner and a status, and it looked like a well-run team. Then the brand lead asked a simple question about one of the packaging concepts: how much of this did AI do, and has anyone checked the claims copy?

Nobody could answer from the board. The designer who owned that line knew. She had the whole thing in a chat thread on her laptop: the brief she pasted in, the twelve rounds of variations, the three she threw out because the product shot drifted off-color. None of it was visible to anyone else in the room. The card just said "In progress."

That moment changed what we worked on for the next two weeks. The team didn't need better prompts that day. They needed the AI work to show up where the rest of the work already lived. Once it did, the numbers followed. Maesa got to an estimated **$280K saved per launch** and started shipping in **three months instead of nine**, across **12+** brands.

## Invisible work can't be managed

Every published a recap this week of their Thesis conference, and one detail stuck with me. Notion's CEO, Ivan Zhao, described his company routing agent work through a shared kanban board so people can see what the agents are actually doing. He also said something I'd put on the wall of every creative department: "I don't think we should surrender our understanding" ([Every, Thesis 2027 recap](https://every.to/context-window/what-you-may-have-missed-at-thesis-2027)).

That's a software company describing a software problem. But creative teams have a sharper version of it, because the thing hidden inside those chat windows is judgment. Which reference the designer chose. Which variation got killed and why. What the model kept getting wrong about the brand, and how she steered it back.

When that stays private, three things break.

**Review breaks.** A creative director can't review what they can't see. They get handed a finished frame with no idea which parts are generated, which are retouched, and which claims came from the brief versus the model's imagination. So they either rubber-stamp it or re-check everything from scratch. Both are expensive.

**Learning breaks.** The designer who figured out how to keep the product color locked across forty variations just solved a problem the whole team has. If that fix lives in her chat history, it dies there. The next person rediscovers it, or doesn't.

**Trust breaks.** This is the quiet one. When leadership can't see AI work, they assume the worst or the best, and neither is useful. I've watched teams lose months because a senior stakeholder found one bad output and nobody could show the forty good ones next to it.

## What "on the board" actually means

I'm not asking anyone to buy a new tool. Most creative teams already have a production tracker, whether it's Asana, Monday, Airtable or a spreadsheet the head of production guards with her life. The job is to make AI work a first-class citizen of that tracker instead of a side activity.

At Maesa that came down to a few unglamorous changes.

**Every card that used AI says so, and says how.** Not a vague "AI-assisted" tag. A line that reads like "base scene generated, product composited by hand, claims copy drafted by model and checked against the approved claims sheet." That one line answers the brand lead's question before anyone asks it.

**The brief and the final prompt get attached to the card.** Not the whole chat transcript, nobody will read that. The inputs that mattered: the reference set, the brand rules that went in, the instruction that finally worked. Now anyone picking up the card next launch starts from the winning setup instead of a blank box.

**Killed variations get one sentence each.** "Product color drifted warm." "Model added a botanical the brand doesn't use." This is the most valuable data on the board, because it's a running list of how this model fails on this brand. Two months of those notes is a better brand guide for AI than anything a vendor will sell you.

**Review has its own column.** AI work doesn't skip the queue because it was fast. It moves to review like everything else, and the reviewer can see what they're signing off on.

None of that is technical. It's production discipline applied to a new kind of work.

## The agents are coming whether the board is ready or not

Here's why I think this matters more now than it did a year ago. Most creative teams are still in the phase where a person drives the AI, so at least there's a human who knows what happened. That's changing fast. The tools are getting good enough to run multi-step jobs on their own: pull the brief, generate the set, resize for every placement, draft the trafficking notes.

When a person runs the AI, hidden work is a nuisance. When an agent runs it, hidden work is a liability. If you can't see what your agents did, you can't review it, you can't fix it, and you definitely can't explain it to a client who asks.

The teams I see getting this right treat agents the way they'd treat a new freelancer. You wouldn't let a freelancer work out of a private folder and drop finished files on the server with no notes. You'd want their work on the board, in the review column, with a paper trail. Same standard.

## This is a whole-team habit, not a power-user trick

The reason this belongs in an operating model and not a tips list is that it only works if everyone does it. One designer documenting her AI work beautifully while five others keep it in private threads gets you nowhere. The board is only useful when it's complete.

That's also why it's one of the first things we look at in an Audit Workshop. I ask the team to pull up their tracker and point at the AI work. Usually they can't. That gap tells me more about where a team really is than any survey about which tools people have licenses for. In the 8-week Operating Model, making AI work visible is one of the habits we install early, because review, reuse and trust all depend on it.

Across the three enterprise teams we've worked with, the pattern has held. The big gains, including the **90%** average production-time reduction, didn't come from someone finding a magic prompt. They came from the team being able to see, check and build on each other's AI work.

So try this on Monday. Open your production tracker in front of the team and ask the brand lead's question: which of this did AI touch, and who checked it? If the room goes quiet, you know what to fix first.
