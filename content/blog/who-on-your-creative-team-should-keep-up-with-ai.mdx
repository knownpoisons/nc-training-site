The first week of the Herman Scheer engagement, someone showed me the team's AI channel in Slack. It was long. Hundreds of links going back months: new model launches, threads from people on X, a tutorial someone had bookmarked twice. Lots of emoji reactions. Almost no replies that said "I tried this on the Tuesday job and here's what happened."

I asked the room who was supposed to be reading all of it. Nobody answered, because the honest answer was everyone. Every designer, producer and strategist had been told, in one way or another, that they needed to stay current. So they all skimmed, they all felt behind, and the agency's actual work didn't change much. A senior producer told me she spent her Sunday evenings watching launch videos so she wouldn't be the one person in Monday's meeting who hadn't heard of the new thing.

That's not an AI strategy. That's a team doing unpaid homework and getting anxious about it.

## Keeping up is real work, so treat it like work

Every's Context Window made this point well this week. Their piece on [keeping up with AI being a full-time job](https://every.to/context-window/keeping-up-with-ai-is-a-full-time-job) describes how they handle it internally: a small frontier team whose job is to test new models and turn the useful experiments into tools for everyone else. The rest of the company gets to stay focused on their actual jobs.

I agree with the diagnosis completely. Keeping up is a job. If you spread it across forty people as a side task, you get forty partial versions of it and none of them finish.

Where I'd push back, at least for creative and media teams, is on the shape of the fix. A dedicated frontier team works when the frontier team builds products and ships them to everyone. Most agencies and in-house brand teams don't have that muscle. What happens instead is that two enthusiasts become "the AI people," they get very good, and the rest of the team learns to forward them questions. You've moved the anxiety off most people's plates, but you've also moved the capability off their plates. When those two leave, and enthusiasts do leave, you're back to the Slack channel.

## Give it to a scout, and rotate the scout

What we set up at Herman Scheer was simpler. One person owns keeping up for a month. Not a specialist, a working producer or designer, with a few hours a week carved out of their billable load so it's not happening on Sunday nights. Next month it's someone else, ideally from a different discipline.

The scout has one filter, and it's strict: **does this change a job we shipped in the last thirty days?** Not "is this impressive." Not "will this matter eventually." If a new image model is better at product renders, the scout reruns three of last month's approved product renders on it and puts the results next to what the client signed off on. If it's better, the team hears about it with proof. If it isn't, nobody hears about it at all.

Everything that doesn't pass the filter gets ignored. That's the part teams find hardest, and it's the part that buys back the most time. Most launches are noise for a creative team in any given month. The scout's real job is to say "not this one" forty times so that everyone else can stop checking.

Then the scout brings the one or two things that passed to a short demo at the team meeting, using the team's own work, not the vendor's demo reel. Ten minutes. Here's the job, here's what I tried, here's what changed, here's the prompt and the references I used. Anyone who wants to try it on a live job that week can.

## Why rotation matters more than expertise

The rotation looks inefficient on paper. You'd get a sharper scout if you picked your best AI person and left them there. But a single permanent scout recreates the two-enthusiasts problem, just with a nicer title.

When the scout changes every month, three things happen. A copywriter scouting will test things a motion designer would never think to check, so the coverage gets wider. Every person who has scouted comes out of it with a working habit of testing against approved work instead of against hype, and that habit stays with them after their month is over. And nobody on the team can opt out of AI by deciding it's someone else's department, because in a few months it will be their department.

This is the difference between AI being a function and AI being the way the team operates. A function lives in a couple of people. An operating model lives in everyone's week. At Herman Scheer the outcome was **$4.5M in estimated savings in year one**, and very little of that came from anyone being the most current person in the industry. It came from a whole team using a small number of techniques on real jobs, consistently, and adding new ones only when they'd proven they beat the old ones.

## How to start this week

You don't need a program to try this. You need four decisions.

**Pick the first scout.** Choose someone curious but busy, not your resident expert. A few protected hours a week, written into their schedule.

**Write the filter down.** "Does this change a job we shipped in the last thirty days?" Pin it in the AI channel, at the top.

**Pick the test set.** Three to five recently approved jobs the scout reruns against anything new. Same references, same brief, side by side with the version the client bought.

**Book the ten minutes.** Same slot every week or every other week. If there's nothing worth showing, the scout says so and you get ten minutes back.

Then tell everyone else, out loud, that they're off the hook. They don't need to watch launch videos on Sunday. They need to use what's already working on this week's jobs, and show up when the scout has something.

Across the three enterprise teams we've worked with, the ones that moved fastest were never the ones that knew the most about AI. They were the ones that had decided who was paying attention, and what they were paying attention for.
