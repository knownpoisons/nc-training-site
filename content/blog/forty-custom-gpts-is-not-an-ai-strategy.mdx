Early in the Cash App engagement, I asked the team to do something slightly awkward. Everyone who had built a custom assistant for their own work, a GPT, a Claude project, a saved prompt doc they swore by, had to open it on the big screen and walk the room through it.

It took most of the afternoon. There were a lot of them. Three different people had built three different "brand voice checkers," each with its own version of the voice rules, and none of the three agreed with the others on how the brand talks about money. One designer had a beautiful resizing helper that nobody else knew existed. A copywriter had an assistant tuned so tightly to her own habits that when a colleague tried it, the output read like an impression of her. And at least two of the assistants on screen belonged to people who had stopped opening them weeks earlier.

Nobody had done anything wrong. That's what enthusiasm looks like when a team gets access and no structure. But I remember thinking that the room was full of individual effort and almost none of it had become team capability. That gap is what we spent the next weeks closing, and it's a big part of how that team got production down to roughly **10%** of what it had been, with an estimated **$3.5 million** in year-one savings.

## Private assistants are a knowledge-hoarding machine

Every's Context Window ran a piece this month on their own version of this. After an offsite, people across the company spun up personal agents, the bots were hard to maintain and hard to keep track of, and most of them quietly died. Their head of platform now argues the company should run [one shared agent instead of a pile of personal ones](https://every.to/context-window/building-a-more-efficient-agent), because a shared agent learns how the company actually works and gets better every time anyone uses it.

That's an engineering-heavy company reaching the same conclusion I've been pushing creative teams toward for two years. And the stakes are higher on a creative team, because the thing those private assistants encode is taste.

When your best art director builds a private assistant that knows how to brief a lighting setup for your hero product, that knowledge lives in her account. When she leaves, it leaves. When she improves it on Tuesday, nobody else gets the improvement. When a junior designer builds his own from scratch, he encodes his current, weaker judgment and then trusts it. You end up with forty assistants that each know a fragment of the brand and one shared brand that none of them fully knows.

That's the opposite of what AI should do for a creative team. The whole promise is that the best person's judgment can travel. A private assistant is the fastest way to make sure it doesn't.

## What we built instead

At Cash App we didn't ban anyone's personal setup. We stopped treating them as the system. The system became a small number of shared assistants, each owned by a named person and each tied to a real stage of production: one for briefs, one for brand voice, one for asset versioning. Everyone used the same ones. When someone found a better instruction or a failure case, it went into the shared version, not their private copy.

Three things changed almost immediately.

First, arguments got useful. When three people have three voice checkers, a disagreement about tone is a disagreement between tools, and nobody wins. When there's one voice assistant, a disagreement becomes a question for the brand lead, she answers it once, and the answer goes into the assistant for everyone.

Second, new people got good faster. A new hire didn't have to spend a month building their own setup or reverse-engineering someone else's. They used the same assistants the senior team used, with the senior team's judgment already in them.

Third, the work started to look like one team made it. That sounds small. It isn't. Consistency is most of what a brand team is paid for, and private assistants quietly erode it.

## The instruction file gets worse before it gets better

Here's the part teams get wrong once they do go shared. A shared assistant attracts rules. Everyone has a pet peeve, so everyone adds a line. The legal note. The banned word. The thing the CMO said once in a review. Six weeks later the instructions run three pages and the output has gotten stiff, hedged and strangely worse.

Every's Katie Parrott wrote about hitting this wall in her own writing setup, adding rules until one essay took 127 drafts, then archiving the whole thing and rebuilding something much smaller. Her advice is the best one-line rule for maintaining a shared assistant I've seen: "Try taking something away before you add another instruction." ([Katie Parrott, via Every's Context Window](https://every.to/context-window/one-agent-for-the-whole-company))

I'd make that a standing practice, not a rescue. On the teams I work with, the owner of each shared assistant does a monthly cut. Every instruction has to earn its place against real recent jobs. If removing a line doesn't make the output worse on the last ten approved pieces, the line goes. Most months, the assistant gets shorter and better at the same time.

## How to start this week

You don't need a platform decision to do this. You need an afternoon and some honesty.

1. **Run the show-and-tell.** Get everyone who has built a personal assistant to show it. You will find duplicates, contradictions, and one or two genuinely brilliant setups nobody knew about.
2. **Pick three stages of your production line** where the team does the most repeated work. Brief writing, brand voice, and versioning are the usual suspects.
3. **Build one shared assistant per stage.** Start from the best personal version you saw, not from scratch. Name an owner for each.
4. **Make improvements flow one way.** Someone finds a better instruction, it goes into the shared assistant. No private forks for team work.
5. **Cut monthly.** Owner tests against recent approved work and removes anything that isn't pulling its weight.

Personal tinkering is still worth encouraging. It's where people find the good stuff. The rule is just that once something works, it stops being yours and starts being the team's.

## The real point

The difference between a team that "uses AI" and a team that has an AI operating model is mostly this. In the first, capability lives in individuals and walks out the door with them. In the second, it lives in shared systems that everyone improves and everyone benefits from. Across the enterprise teams I've worked with, that shift, more than any model or tool, is what drove an average production-time reduction of around **90%**.

If your team's AI knowledge currently lives in forty private accounts, that's not a failure. It's raw material. The half-day Audit Workshop is where we usually find it and sort the brilliant from the duplicated. The 8-week Operating Model is where we turn it into the shared system.
