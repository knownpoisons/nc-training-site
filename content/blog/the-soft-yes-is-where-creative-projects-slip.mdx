Midway through the Cash App engagement, I sat in on a post-mortem for a campaign that had shipped two days late. Nobody had dropped the ball in the project plan. Every task in the tracker was green right up until it wasn't.

We went back through the Slack history together. The slip started with one message from three weeks earlier. A designer had asked a copywriter if she could look at the alt lines for the social cutdowns "sometime this week." She replied "yep, on it." That was it. It never made it into the tracker, because it didn't feel like a task. It felt like a favor. Then the week got busy, the favor evaporated, and the cutdowns sat waiting on copy nobody knew was owed.

I've now seen some version of that thread at every team I've worked with. The plan holds the big commitments. The soft yes, the "I'll take a look," the "send it over and I'll give you notes tomorrow," lives in chat and in people's heads. On a creative team, that's where most of the actual handoffs happen.

## The commitments that never become tickets

Producers know this already. The good ones keep a second, private list of everything they've heard someone agree to, and they chase it. That list is one of the most valuable things on the team, and it lives in one person's notebook.

Every's Context Window ran a piece this week from a writer in [his first week with an AI coworker](https://every.to/context-window/my-first-week-with-an-ai-coworker), the company agent that sits in their Slack. One detail stuck with me. A colleague of his has her agent keep a running list of the requests she agreed to casually in Slack or email, then nudge her before the deadline she implied.

That's the producer's notebook, automated. And it's the first agent use case I'd hand to a creative team that's still nervous about agents, because it doesn't touch the craft at all. It doesn't write a headline or generate a frame. It just remembers what people said they'd do.

## How I'd set it up on a creative team

The version that helps one person is nice. The version that helps the whole team is where the value is. Here's how I'd run it.

**Make it shared, not personal.** If each person runs their own private tracker, you've rebuilt the producer's notebook forty times and nobody can see across them. Put one agent in the channels where the work actually gets negotiated: the project channels, the review threads, the client-feedback channel. Every soft yes in those channels gets logged against a person and a rough date.

**Give it one source of truth and tell it which one.** The Every piece also describes the agent confidently telling someone their approved time off was still pending, because it read an old record instead of the shared tracker. On a creative team, that failure looks like the agent telling a producer the hero image is still in round two because it found last week's thread. So be explicit: the tracker wins, the latest approved file wins, and if the agent finds a conflict it flags it instead of picking one.

**Send the nudge to the person who promised, not to their manager.** This one matters for adoption. If the agent becomes a snitch, people stop saying yes in public and start saying it in DMs, and you've lost the visibility you were after. The nudge goes to the person who made the promise, a day before it's due. The weekly roll-up goes to the producer as a list of open handoffs, not a list of who's late.

**Review it in the Monday meeting.** Five minutes. What's open, what's stale, what was quietly dropped and should be officially dropped. Saying "that one's dead" out loud is half the value.

## The part an agent can't do for you

The same Every piece cites research that people working with AI tend to adopt its suggested approach and repeat its arguments as their own. I see the creative version of this in almost every team I train. Someone accepts the AI's read on a brief, presents it in the review, and can't explain why it's right when the creative director pushes back.

A commitment tracker has a quieter version of the same risk. If the agent decides what counts as a promise, the team stops deciding. So the rule I give teams is simple: the agent proposes, a human confirms. When it logs "Sam owes alt lines by Thursday," Sam gets to say "yes," "no, that was a maybe," or "move it to Monday." Ownership stays with the people. The agent just makes sure nothing falls out of the conversation unnoticed.

## Why this belongs in the operating model, not a side project

None of this is about a specific tool. Every always-on agent your team can buy or build in the next year will be able to read a channel and keep a list. The technique is what matters: decide which channels count, decide what the source of truth is, decide who gets nudged and when, and review it as a team every week.

That's the difference between AI as something two people on the team play with and AI as part of how the whole team works. At Cash App, production came down to roughly **10%** of what it had been, with **$3.5 million** in estimated year-one savings and **30%** more output. Most of that came from rebuilding how the team made and reviewed work. But a lot of the days we got back weren't in the making at all. They were in the gaps between people, the handoffs that slipped because a "yep, on it" never turned into anything anyone could see.

If your team's real schedule lives in Slack, start there. Pick one project channel, put an agent on it for two weeks, and see how many soft yeses it catches that your tracker never knew about. That number will tell you more about your production risk than any status report.
