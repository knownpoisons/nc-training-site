Early in the Cash App engagement, I sat with the copy team while they ran a batch of social lines through the AI setup they'd built. The setup looked responsible. At the top of every prompt sat the brand's tone-of-voice summary: confident, playful, a little irreverent, never corny. Four adjectives and a warning.

The lines that came back were all four adjectives at once. Every one of them winked. One had a pun about money "growing on trees." The writer next to me read it out loud, and the room groaned in the specific way a creative team groans when something is almost on brand and therefore worse than off brand.

I asked the creative lead a question that turned out to matter more than any prompt we wrote that month: where are the lines you killed? She had them. Every review deck from the last two quarters was sitting in a shared drive, with the shipped lines and, two slides earlier, the versions that got cut. Nobody had ever shown those to the model. That afternoon we started, and it ended up being one of the habits behind the team cutting production time to roughly **10% of what it used to be**, with **30% more output** and an estimated **$3.5M saved in year one**.

## Adjectives are a description, not a standard

Every brand book I've seen has a voice page, and almost every voice page is a list of adjectives. Bold. Warm. Human. Smart but not smug. They're useful for people, because people fill in the gaps with years of context. A writer who has sat through fifty reviews knows what "playful" means at this company and where it tips into try-hard.

A model has none of that. Give it "playful" and it reaches for the average of every playful thing it has ever read, which is how you end up with money growing on trees. The adjectives aren't wrong. They're just not a standard you can hit. They describe the target without showing where the edges are.

Every's Context Window ran a piece this week on the same problem from the writing side. Their head of evals got AI to sound like him by [testing drafts against his published posts, not describing his style](https://every.to/context-window/stop-telling-ai-how-you-write-show-it). That's an individual writer working on his own voice. On a creative team it goes further, because a brand voice isn't one person's taste. It's dozens of reviews, by several people, over years. The good news is that most teams have already written all of it down. They just filed it as old decks.

## The near-miss is the lesson

Here's what I'd push you on. Showing the model your best approved work helps, but it isn't the big win. The big win is the pairs.

A shipped line on its own tells the model what good looks like. A shipped line next to the version that got killed tells it where the line is. "Playful" stops being a vibe and becomes a decision: this joke, yes; this joke, too much. "Confident" becomes: this claim, fine; this claim, legal will call. That boundary is the thing your creative director carries in their head and the thing no adjective captures.

At Cash App, we pulled a set of these pairs out of the review decks. Each one got three parts: the brief it answered, the line that died, the line that shipped. Then one short sentence on why, in the creative lead's words, not ours. "Too pleased with itself." "Says the feature, doesn't say why anyone cares." "Funny, but it's funny about us, not about them."

Those one-line reasons were gold. They were the notes the team had been giving out loud for two years.

## How to build it on your team

You don't need anything fancy for this. You need a few hours and the decks you already have.

**Pull 20 to 30 pairs from real reviews.** Go back through the last two quarters. For each one, grab the brief, the killed version, and the shipped version. Pick a spread of formats: headlines, social, scripts, product copy. If you only pull headlines, the model will only get headlines right.

**Write the reason in one sentence.** Have the person who made the call write it, or at least approve it. Keep their wording. "Too corporate" in your CD's voice is worth more than a paragraph of polished explanation from someone else.

**Put the pairs where the whole team's AI can reach them.** Not in one writer's custom setup. A shared project, a shared skill file, whatever your team actually uses. If only your best writer has it, you've made your best writer faster and left everyone else guessing. This is the part teams skip, and it's why AI ends up working for two people instead of the department.

**Hold some pairs back and test.** Keep five or six pairs out of the set. Give the model those briefs cold and see whether its draft lands closer to the shipped line or the killed one. If it keeps landing on the killed side, you've learned something about which boundary isn't coming through, and you add pairs for exactly that.

**Add to it every review.** Every time a line dies in review, it's a candidate. The set should grow as fast as your creative director gives notes. Ten minutes at the end of each review is plenty.

## What changes when you do this

The first thing you notice is that draft one gets closer. Not perfect. Closer. Writers stop spending the first hour of a job sanding the corny out of AI output and start working from something that's already in the right neighborhood.

The second thing is quieter and matters more. Juniors get the pairs too. A writer three months into the account now has two years of review decisions sitting next to them, with reasons. They learn the voice faster, from the same material the model learns it from. The pairs are a training asset for people, not just a prompt.

The third thing is that your voice stops depending on who's in the room. When a creative lead goes on leave or moves on, the boundary they drew doesn't leave with them. It's written down, in pairs, in their words.

None of this is a tool. It's a technique, and it works on whatever model you're running this year and whatever replaces it next year. The adjectives on your voice page can stay. Just stop expecting them to do the job of the decks you already have.
