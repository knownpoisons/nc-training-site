About a month into the Herman Scheer engagement, I sat in on a Thursday creative review. The creative director had a stack of AI-assisted concept frames on the screen for a hospitality client, and on the third frame she said, "The light's too clean again. Real lobbies have a little mess in them." Two frames later: "Same thing. It looks like a render."

After the review I asked the designer how many times he'd heard that note. He scrolled back through Slack and counted. Eleven, across four projects and three different designers. Every one of them had fixed it on their own frames, in their own chat window, and then started the next job from zero. The note had been given eleven times and saved zero times.

That's when we stopped working on prompts for a while and started working on her notes. The agency went on to an estimated **$4.5M in year-one savings**, and a real piece of that came from one boring habit: no creative note gets given twice.

## Your taste lives in your corrections

Every's Context Window ran a piece this week on what they call [the age of artisanal AI](https://every.to/context-window/the-age-of-artisanal-ai-is-coming). The argument is that when every team has access to the same models, the default output gets cheap, and the only thing left that's actually yours is your judgment about what good looks like. They point out that today, a model only reflects your taste if you keep feeding it that taste, over and over. They're looking a year or two out, at models you can shape directly with your saved corrections.

I agree with the diagnosis, and I don't think creative teams should wait for the future version. You can do the useful part now, with tools you already pay for.

Here's the thing most teams miss. Your taste isn't written down in the brand book. The brand book says "warm, human, premium." That's not taste, that's adjectives. Your taste shows up in the moment a creative director looks at something and says no, and then says why. "Too clean." "The hands look staged." "That headline is clever but it's not us." Every one of those is a rule. Most teams throw them away the moment the frame gets fixed.

## What we built instead

At Herman Scheer, it was not a fancy system. It was a shared correction log, plus a habit.

**One place for every note.** Any time a creative director or senior creative gave a note on AI-assisted work, the person receiving it added one line to a shared doc: the note in plain words, a before and after if there was one, and which clients it applied to. Thirty seconds. The rule was simple. If you got a note, you logged it before you fixed it.

**Notes become instructions.** Once a week, someone on the team spent an hour turning the raw notes into instructions the AI could actually use. "Too clean" became something specific: lived-in spaces, a coffee cup on the counter, uneven daylight, nothing that looks freshly staged. Those instructions went into the project setup every designer started from, not into one person's saved prompts.

**Notes become checks.** This is the part that saved the creative director's week. Before anything went to her, the designer ran the frames past the same model with the correction log attached and asked it a blunt question: does this break any rule on this list? It caught the obvious repeats. She stopped seeing "too clean" in review because it got filtered out before review.

**Notes get retired.** Some corrections were about one campaign and shouldn't follow the team forever. Every month we pruned the log. A note that hadn't fired in two months either got folded into the general guidance or deleted.

## Why this has to be the whole team

The failure mode I see most is that one enthusiastic designer builds a beautiful personal prompt library with all the creative director's preferences baked in. His work gets better. Nobody else's does. Then he leaves, and the library leaves with him.

A correction log only works if everyone writes to it and everyone reads from it. The junior designer who started last week should inherit four months of the creative director's judgment on day one. That's the actual value. You're not just saving one person's time in review. You're turning one senior person's taste into something the whole team can use without booking time on her calendar.

It also changes what the creative director spends her attention on. Every's team made a related point in another piece this week, that [your attention is AI's new bottleneck](https://every.to/context-window/your-attention-is-ai-s-new-bottleneck). On a creative team, the scarcest attention is the senior person's review time. If she's spending it on the same eleven notes, you're wasting the most expensive hour in the building. When the repeats get caught upstream, her review time goes to the decisions only she can make: is this the right idea, is this the right story, should we kill it.

## Where to start Monday

You don't need a tool for this. You need a doc and a rule.

1. Pick one client or one workstream. Don't try to capture the whole agency's taste at once.
2. Create the log. Note, before and after, scope. That's the whole template.
3. Make it a rule that notes get logged before they get fixed. The creative director has to back this, or it dies in a week.
4. Spend one hour every Friday turning notes into instructions and adding them to the shared project setup.
5. Before anything goes to review, run it against the log.

Within a month you'll know something useful: which of your notes are real brand rules and which were just one person's mood on a Tuesday. Both are worth knowing.

This is the kind of thing we map in the half-day Audit Workshop: where your team's judgment is leaking out, and which of those leaks are costing you the most review time. In the 8-week Operating Model, we build it into how the whole team works, so the habit outlasts the people who started it. Across the three enterprise teams we've worked with, the production-time cuts averaged around **90%**, and none of that sticks if the senior people are still giving the same notes every Thursday.

Your taste is the one thing the model can't download. Stop letting it evaporate in Slack.
