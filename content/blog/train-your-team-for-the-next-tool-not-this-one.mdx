A few weeks into the Maesa engagement, the image model half the team relied on shipped an update. Overnight, the outputs looked different. Skin tones shifted, the lighting got flatter, and the packaging renders that had looked right on Friday looked slightly off on Monday.

I watched the room split in two. One group had memorized a set of prompts that worked. Those prompts stopped working, and those designers spent the morning in a group chat trying to reverse-engineer what changed. The other group had learned how to work: start from approved references, describe the job the image has to do, compare every output against the last campaign the brand director signed off on. They adjusted by lunch. Same team, same tool, same update. The only difference was what we had trained.

That team went on to cut launches from nine months to three, at roughly **$280K saved per launch** across **12+ brands**. None of that would have held if every model update reset the team to zero.

## Founders pivot. So do your tools.

Every's Context Window ran a piece this week with investor Matt Hartman, who assumes almost every startup he backs will pivot. So he doesn't bet on the plan. He bets on founders with a skill they can point at a new market and the ability to, in his words, ["move with where the world goes."](https://every.to/context-window/the-best-founders-have-something-ai-can-t-tell-them) He also looks for what he calls earned insight, the knowledge you only get by being in the founder's seat.

I read that and thought about creative teams, because the logic carries over almost exactly. You should assume the tools your team uses today will be replaced, renamed, repriced or quietly made worse within a year. I've watched it happen to every client I've worked with. So the question isn't which tool to train on. It's what your team knows that survives the next swap.

Most AI training gets this backwards. It's a tour of the interface. Here's where the button is, here's the prompt template, here's the setting you change. It feels productive because everyone leaves with something they can do on Tuesday. Then the vendor moves the button, and you're booking another session.

## What actually transfers

When I say techniques over tools, I mean something specific. These are the habits I see hold up across every model change, every vendor switch, every new app someone's cousin sent them.

**Start from references, not adjectives.** "Premium, clean, aspirational" means something different to every model and every designer. Three approved images and one sentence about what they have in common means the same thing everywhere. Teams that brief from references get consistent work from whatever tool is open.

**Judge against approved work.** Before anyone decides an output is good, they hold it next to the last thing the client actually bought. That habit works whether you're generating images, copy or cutdowns. It also means a model update gets caught in an hour instead of a week.

**Break the job into steps.** A finished asset is never one prompt. It's research, then options, then a pick, then refinement, then a check. People who think in steps can rebuild the chain in any tool. People who think in one magic prompt can't.

**Write down what worked.** Not in someone's head and not in a private assistant. In a shared place the next person can open. When the tool changes, you rerun the documented process and see what breaks.

None of these are about a product. That's the point. You can teach them on whatever your team has access to this month, and they'll still be true when it has something else.

## Earned insight is the part no model can give you

The second half of Hartman's idea matters even more for creative teams. A model can produce a hundred hero shots. It can't tell you that this client's founder hates the color orange, that the last campaign died in legal because of one claim on the pack, or that "make it pop" from this brand director means more negative space, not more saturation.

That's your team's earned insight. It came from years of approvals, rejections and awkward review calls. It's the real asset, and in most teams it lives in three senior people's heads.

The teams that get the most out of AI put that insight into the work. It goes into the brief, the references, the checklist the output gets judged against. When that's done, a junior designer with a good model produces work that sounds like your senior team. When it isn't, the same designer produces work that sounds like everyone else using that model.

This is also why I push so hard on installing AI across the whole team instead of two enthusiasts. Earned insight is spread out. The producer knows which retailer rejects which crop. The copywriter knows which phrases the client's legal team flags. If only two people are using AI, only their slice of the insight gets in.

## A quick test for your team

Here's a drill I run early in most engagements. Pick a real job your team shipped last month. Hand it to two people, but make them use a tool they haven't used before. Give them a morning.

If the result is close to what you shipped, your team has techniques. If they spend the morning lost in settings, your team has tool habits. Neither result is a failure. It just tells you what to train next.

Across the three enterprise teams I've worked with, the gains that lasted, around **90% average reduction in production time**, came from the teams that passed this test by the end. Not because they found the best tool. Because they stopped depending on any one of them.
